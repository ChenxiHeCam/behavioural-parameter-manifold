\begin{abstract}
Mechanistic models can reproduce biological outputs while leaving their underlying parameters uncertain. We quantify how sensitivity concentrates across parameter combinations using complete spectra, matched comparisons of measured outputs and model states, and fitting experiments with known generating parameters. In a nematode circuit with $3076$ connection weights, $26$ combinations carry $90\%$ of motor-voltage sensitivity and $66$ carry $99\%$. The concentration estimate changes with output reduction and perturbation scale in a walking controller, and across outputs derived from the same nematode connection perturbations. In a matched fly visual-network comparison, sensitivity is more concentrated in the trained state than in the initialized state. In a separate gain assay, combining stimulation conditions records additional sensitivity directions while the spectrum remains strongly concentrated. In cell models with known generating parameters, a concentrated sensitivity spectrum accompanies both accurate recovery and output-matched fits with substantial parameter error. A prospective simulated stimulus comparison did not demonstrate an average prediction benefit from the tested ensemble-selection rule. Sensitivity concentration alone can miss directions added by a new assay and cannot specify achieved parameter accuracy.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}
Mechanistic models connect biological components to the outputs they collectively generate. A neural circuit model maps synaptic weights and membrane conductances to activity; a biochemical pathway maps rate constants to a signalling response. Fitting these outputs makes a model useful for reproducing observations and testing perturbations. It also raises a question: what does the measured output reveal about the model's internal parameters?

Different parameter sets can produce similar dynamics. Conductance-based neural models provide well-studied examples of this degeneracy \citep{prinz2004similar,goldman2001global,marder2006variability,marder2011variability}. Sloppy-model theory describes a related feature: output sensitivity can differ greatly between parameter combinations, so that a few strongly sensitive combinations dominate a much larger space \citep{brown2003statistical,gutenkunst2007universally,transtrum2015perspective}. This concentration is distinct from identifiability, which concerns whether observations determine parameter values under a specified model and inference protocol \citep{raue2009identifiability,villaverde2014reverse,chis2016sloppiness,white2016limitations}. Large pathway models can retain useful predictive accuracy despite substantial parameter uncertainty \citep{kreutz2012prediction,froehlich2018efficient}.

Connectome-constrained models now make it possible to examine this distinction at the scale of hundreds of neurons and thousands of connection weights. Wiring diagrams \citep{white1986structure,cook2019connectome,dorkenwald2024flywire}, biophysical simulation frameworks \citep{hines1997neuron,szigeti2014openworm,gleeson2018c302}, and measured kinematics \citep{javer2018open,pereira2020quantifying} support models of nematode movement and fly sensorimotor function \citep{wang2025baaiworm,wangchen2024neuromechfly,lobatorios2022neuromechfly,lappalainen2024connectome}. Their outputs can be perturbed repeatedly while controlling the parameters, input and readout. Neural activity, motor commands and body movement nevertheless remain different measurements of the same underlying system. These models provide a setting to examine whether changing an assay reveals additional sensitivity directions, changes their concentration or improves parameter recovery.

Here, we quantify concentration from the complete connection-weight spectrum of a nematode circuit and compare outputs derived from the same perturbations. Matched measurements of a walking controller and a fly visual network test how readout and model state change that concentration. By combining stimulation conditions, we test whether added sensitivity directions are captured by the concentration count. Known-truth recovery experiments then examine whether a similar count implies similar parameter accuracy. A prospective stimulus comparison tests whether choosing an additional observation from a baseline-fitted model ensemble improves prediction.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Output sensitivity at connectome scale}
We measure the change in a specified output vector when each parameter is perturbed in turn. The resulting finite-difference Jacobian $J$ records how parameter changes map onto output changes. A fixed diagonal weighting $W$ standardizes those changes, and the Gram matrix $H=J^{\top}WJ$ summarizes squared sensitivity along parameter combinations. The counts $d_{90}$ and $d_{99}$ are the smallest numbers of leading combinations carrying $90\%$ and $99\%$ of the total squared sensitivity. We call these concentration counts. Numerically resolved directions have singular values above a stated numerical tolerance.

The primary assay uses the BAAIWorm point-neuron circuit, with $136$ cells and $3076$ chemical-synapse and gap-junction weights. A fixed sinusoidal sensory drive supplies the input, and $80$ motor-neuron voltages sampled over time supply $9600$ output components. At relative perturbation step $0.5$, $d_{90}=26$ and $d_{99}=66$, with participation ratio $6.83$, a continuous measure of the effective number of contributing sensitivity directions (Fig.~\ref{fig:concentration}b). Thus, fewer than $1\%$ of parameter combinations carry $90\%$ of the squared motor-voltage response. Perturbation-step controls distinguish strong sampled responses from an unresolved weak response (Supplementary~\S2).

Chemical synapses contribute $69\%$ of squared motor-voltage sensitivity from $1992$ weights, compared with $31\%$ from $1084$ gap-junction weights. The mean squared sensitivity per chemical connection is $1.22$ times the gap-junction value, while the median column norm, measuring response magnitude per connection, is $6.62$ times greater. The difference between these ratios shows that typical connection sensitivity and total class contribution describe different aspects of the distribution.

\subsection*{Dependence on the output and perturbation scale}
Reducing the output vector can hide sensitivity directions before any inference is attempted. A Jacobian with $n$ output components has rank at most $n$, regardless of the number of parameters. Conversely, many time samples need not supply independent information. We examined this dependence using the same $48$ actuator gains of the FlyGym walking controller \citep{wangchen2024neuromechfly}. At a relative perturbation step of $0.25$, ten summary statistics give $d_{90}=1$ and $d_{99}=1$, whereas $4200$ joint-angle samples give $11$ and $31$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:measurement}a). This comparison changes the recorded output while keeping the parameter set and step fixed.

Perturbation size also changes the measured spectrum. Across relative steps of $0.05$, $0.15$, $0.25$ and $0.50$, the joint-angle response divided by the step is $1.98$, $1.39$, $1.34$ and $1.31$. The corresponding $d_{90}$ values are $16$, $12$, $11$ and $11$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:measurement}b,c). The smallest step emphasizes realization variability (Supplementary~\S2); the two largest steps give similar response-to-step ratios and spectral concentration. At $0.50$, some simulations diverge and two gains have no usable perturbation pair, so we use $0.25$ for the readout comparison.

The same full-connectome perturbations also change their apparent concentration across the model's output layers. The linear projection of motor voltages into muscle outputs, before clipping, gives concentration counts $3/9$, while the model's clipped activation gives $5/17$, compared with motor-voltage $26/66$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:concentration}b). Each readout uses its own fixed baseline temporal scales. Thus the output layer and its normalization alter the measured concentration, even with the connection perturbations held fixed.

\subsection*{Mechanisms contributing to motor-output sensitivity}
We next replaced individual connection weights with sixteen named conductance gains, each applied uniformly to cells expressing the corresponding mechanism. The resulting coordinates represent mechanism classes, rather than independent per-cell conductances. Their finite-step motor-voltage response has $d_{90}=3$ and $d_{99}=6$, and all sixteen gains produce a measurable response. The inward rectifier IRK, sodium leak NCA and potassium conductances EGL-36 and EGL-2 have the largest column norms. EGL-19 and UNC-2 are relatively weak under this input and voltage readout (Supplementary~\S3).

The eigenvectors distinguish class sensitivity from combinations of classes. For a normalized eigenvector, participation is the inverse sum of fourth powers of its loadings: it describes the number of coordinates contributing appreciably to that direction. The leading direction has participation $2.88$ across the sixteen coordinates, with NCA providing the largest loading; the weakest direction has participation $1.02$ and is dominated by SLO-1/EGL-19. Thus, strongly sensitive combinations involve a few conductance classes, while some weak combinations are close to single classes.

We also measured sensitivity at nearby parameter points. Using an identical $300$-connection subset at the reference point and four displaced points, $d_{90}$ ranges from $8$ to $12$ and $d_{99}$ from $28$ to $30$. Leading subspaces have mean pairwise principal-angle cosine $0.89$, compared with $0.16$ in a matched random-subspace control. Concentration persists across these sampled points (Fig.~\ref{fig:concentration}e). Its rollout and output weighting differ from those of the full-connectome measurement.

\subsection*{Visual-network state and sensitivity concentration}
The connectome-constrained flyvis network provides a sensory-circuit comparison \citep{lappalainen2024connectome}. We load one pretrained network and compare it with the library's default initialized state. Its $734$ parameters comprise $65$ biases, $65$ time constants and $604$ synaptic strengths. Positive time constants and strengths use log coordinates, while biases use a fixed affine scale. The trained network has $31$ zero-strength boundary coordinates. We exclude these zero-strength connections from both states, leaving the same $703$ parameter coordinates; output weights are fixed to the trained baseline.

We average activity over the final five stimulus frames and group contiguous neuron indices into bins. With $64$ neural-activity bins, the trained network gives $d_{90}=9$ and $d_{99}=22$, compared with $15$ and $34$ in the initialized network (Fig.~\ref{fig:visual}a). All $64$ readout directions are numerically resolved under the stated tolerance, illustrating the difference between rank and concentration. Reducing the recorded bins changes both counts (Fig.~\ref{fig:visual}b). Both states receive the same sinusoidal luminance movie and use the same output metric.

Finite-difference and integration-step controls preserve strong concentration while exposing discrepancies in weak responses (Fig.~\ref{fig:visual}c; Supplementary~\S4). The implementation's time-constant floor changes the effective dynamics when the integration step is reduced.

\subsection*{Stimulation conditions and combined sensitivity}
Changing what a circuit is driven to do can change which perturbations affect its output. In a closed-loop BAAIWorm assay recording path statistics, chemosensory gain perturbations produce standardized output changes of $0.872$ with food close and $0.122$ with food far, a $7.15$-fold contrast. Motor gain perturbations show the opposite ordering, $0.109$ and $0.357$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:context}a).

Combining stimulation conditions exposes additional numerically resolved sensitivity directions. For $20$ gains, the two conditions have $4$ and $4$ resolved sensitivity directions, respectively, at relative singular-value tolerance $10^{-6}$, while the stacked assay has $8$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:context}b). Its concentration counts are $2/4$ for chemotaxis, $1/2$ for locomotion and $2/4$ for the combined assay. The $14$ neuron-class leak gains similarly give $4/4/8$ resolved directions, with combined concentration $1/4$. For the twenty-gain comparison, the combined assay records more resolved sensitivity directions even though its concentration counts equal those of chemotaxis.

Within the FlyGym walking controller, sensitivity remains concentrated across the four descending commands tested. Averaging over all subsets of a given size, $d_{90}$ increases from $12.25$ for individual commands to $13.00$ for all four; $d_{99}$ increases from $30.75$ to $32.00$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:context}c). These combined conditions change the concentration counts little.

\subsection*{Prospective stimulus comparison}
We next tested whether a set of models fitted to a baseline observation could guide the choice of an additional stimulus and improve prediction. In $12$ independent simulated parameter cases, each strategy observed one noisy baseline and one noisy additional voltage trajectory. The strategy selects a stimulus using a Gaussian score of predicted response variation across baseline-fitted models relative to observation noise. Controls repeat the baseline, select a random candidate or select the largest-response candidate. Mean voltage root-mean-square error (RMSE) on the excluded stimulus was $0.115$ mV for ensemble selection, compared with $0.053$, $0.049$ and $0.039$ mV for the three controls. All paired descriptive bootstrap intervals for the mean differences crossed zero. This experiment does not demonstrate an average predictive improvement from the selection rule (Supplementary~\S8).

\subsection*{Sensitivity concentration and parameter recovery}
Cell models let us compare a sensitivity measurement with recovery of known generating parameters. We examined eleven models spanning neuronal excitability and biochemical oscillations. Starts are random multiplicative displacements around the generating parameter vector, with fold widths $1.5$, $3$ and $10$. For each model, we report both the fraction of fits whose output error falls below $0.02$ and the achieved parameter error among those fits (Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery}b,d). Parameter error is a normalized Euclidean distance in the model's stated units.

Successful output fits give accurate parameter recovery in some models and substantial error in others. In the Hodgkin--Huxley panel, all $72$ starts meet the output threshold, with median normalized parameter error $1.85\times10^{-5}$. Morris--Lecar and FitzHugh--Nagumo also give small errors among successful fits. In contrast, MAPK reaches the output threshold in $5$ of $24$ starts, yet its median parameter error among those fits is $0.40$.

The Hodgkin--Huxley assay grid separates concentration from achieved recovery more directly. We vary the free parameter count ($3$, $5$ or $8$) and record either one spike-count statistic, seven current--frequency and spike-shape features, or voltage traces with $200$ or $1000$ samples. The measured $d_{90}$ is $1$ in all twelve combinations. For the seven-feature assay, median parameter error rises from $3.8\%$ with three free parameters to $38\%$ with eight, while median output error remains below $0.02$. In the trace-based combinations, the median fit fails the output threshold, so its parameter error also reflects optimization failure (Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery}c). A single concentration count therefore cannot specify how many parameters a fitting procedure will recover.

\section*{Discussion}
Output sensitivity is strongly concentrated in the connectome-scale nematode circuit under the measured input and readout. Individual weight perturbations change motor-neuron voltage, yet a small set of combinations carries most of the squared response. Comparisons of conductance gains and connection classes locate the components contributing to that response. Neural voltages, muscle readouts, joint angles and path statistics expose different aspects of a model, and reducing those outputs can reduce the span of sensitivity before parameter fitting begins. The spectrum depends on parameter coordinates and output normalization as well as the model and readout.

A concentration count describes relative sensitivity, whereas parameter recovery also depends on its magnitude and the precision of observations. Multiplying every eigenvalue by the same positive factor leaves the concentration counts unchanged, even though all output responses become stronger. Weak directions can still be estimated from sufficiently precise data. Conversely, a poor fit can leave parameters inaccurate even when the observation map contains information about them. The cell-model experiments connect these distinctions to achieved parameter accuracy, extending work on sloppiness and inference \citep{gutenkunst2007universally,white2016limitations,froehlich2018efficient}.

The span of resolved sensitivity directions can expand while $d_{90}$ remains nearly unchanged, because additional directions may carry little weight compared with the leading one. The complete twenty-gain nematode spectra provide a direct example: the combined assay resolves more directions than either condition alone while retaining the chemotaxis concentration counts. The FlyGym command set likewise retains concentrated combined sensitivity. These findings motivate examining both the directions supplied by a new assay and their magnitudes. Iterative experimental design has improved parameter estimation in biochemical models \citep{sethna2010sloppy,transtrum2012selection,balsacanto2010identification}. Ensemble selection did not demonstrate an average predictive improvement in our simulated comparison, which used an observation-noise model and an excluded prediction condition. The value of a new measurement therefore also depends on the model ensemble, fitting procedure and measurement cost.

Biological interpretation requires relating model parameters to experimental perturbations. Class-level sensitivity rankings concern uniform model gains; gene-level perturbations can change multiple mechanisms and cells. Exploratory comparisons with two fly connectomes and a nematode signal-propagation atlas did not support transfer of the sensitivity rankings to those animal measurements (Supplementary~\S6). Postural variation, electrophysiological heterogeneity and sequence conservation provide separate biological context, with distinct observables and levels of description. The numerical interpretation is likewise specific to the measured perturbation scale: some weak, oscillator and locomotor responses do not admit a validated local derivative.

\section*{Methods}
\subsection*{Model systems and measurements}
The primary system is the point-neuron circuit derived from BAAIWorm \citep{wang2025baaiworm}, simulated with NEURON \citep{hines1997neuron}. Its $136$ cells and $3076$ connection weights are driven by fixed sensory input. The full-connectome assay records $80$ motor-neuron voltages; closed-loop body trajectories are assessed in the separate context experiments. Conductance assays perturb named gains uniformly across expressing cells. FlyGym \citep{wangchen2024neuromechfly} provides actuator-gain measurements from summary statistics and joint angles. The flyvis visual model \citep{lappalainen2024connectome} is described with its explicit network state and coordinates in Supplementary~\S4. Cell-model equations and fitting protocols are specified in Supplementary~\S5; other systems are assessed in Supplementary~\S2 and \S7.

\subsection*{Parameter coordinates and sensitivity matrix}
For nonzero connection weights and positive gains we use dimensionless relative coordinates, implemented either as a multiplicative central perturbation $\theta_i(1\pm\delta)$ or as $\theta_i e^{\pm\delta}$. Signs of inhibitory weights are held fixed, so their local coordinates correspond to log magnitude. At zero displacement the two perturbation schemes have the same derivative; finite-step estimates need not coincide. Signed or zero-valued flyvis parameters use the separately specified affine or one-sided coordinates. The raw Jacobian $J$ is standardized as $K=W^{1/2}J$ using fixed reference output scales. The sensitivity matrix is $H=K^{\top}K=J^{\top}WJ$. Here, $W$ encodes the fixed reference output scales.

For coordinates $q$, the sensitivity matrix is $H=J_q^{\top}WJ_q$, with non-negative eigenvalues up to floating-point error. For a locally linear transformation $q=Tz$, $H_z=T^{\top}H_qT$, so concentration depends on parameter scaling. The concentration count is
\[
d_\alpha=\min\left\{k:\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i\ge\alpha\sum_i\lambda_i\right\},
\]
where eigenvalues are ordered from largest to smallest. We report $d_{90}$ and $d_{99}$, and the participation ratio $(\sum_i\lambda_i)^2/\sum_i\lambda_i^2$. Numerical rank is reported separately using an explicit tolerance on a complete spectrum. The number of output components bounds the number of sensitivity directions that an assay can record; other parameter directions can affect other readouts.

\subsection*{Numerical diagnostics and combined conditions}
We compare repeat baselines, response norms divided by perturbation step, and derivative estimates across steps where available. Repeat baselines assess realization variability under identical settings, and step comparisons assess changes in the finite-difference estimates. A roughly constant response-to-step ratio supports a finite-step interpretation over that tested range. Discontinuous or phase-sensitive responses are described separately from validated differential measurements. For matched parameter coordinates, combined conditions use $H_{\mathrm{combined}}=\sum_c H_c$ with the stated per-condition weights and fixed baseline output scales. The rank of this sum describes the span of the recorded directions; its concentration counts describe their relative contributions.

\subsection*{Known-truth parameter fitting}
The eleven cell models include Hodgkin--Huxley, its A-current variant, Wang--Buzs\'aki, Morris--Lecar, FitzHugh--Nagumo, MAPK, repressilator, Goodwin, Selkov, Brusselator and Goldbeter oscillators \citep{hodgkin1952quantitative,connor1971prediction,wang1996gamma,morris1981voltage,fitzhugh1961impulses,kholodenko2000negative,elowitz2000synthetic,goodwin1965oscillatory,selkov1968self,prigogine1968symmetry,goldbeter1991minimal}. Starts satisfy $\theta_i^{\mathrm{init}}=\theta_i^*e^{\eta_i}$, with independent $\eta_i\sim\mathcal U(-\ln f,\ln f)$ and $f\in\{1.5,3,10\}$. Thus, random displacements are centered on the known truth. Features are computed on settled traces at full resolution and then reduced. The fitting loss combines amplitude quantiles, Fourier magnitudes and autocorrelation features, normalized by the mean absolute target-feature value. Fits below loss $0.02$ supply the primary conditional recovery summaries. Parameter error is $\|\hat\theta-\theta^*\|_2/\|\theta^*\|_2$ in the model's stated units; fit fractions are reported alongside it. The assay grid additionally reports medians across all starts, with unsuccessful output matching marked explicitly.

\subsection*{Statistics and biological context}
Displaced-point subspaces are compared through principal-angle cosines and a matched random-orientation control. FlyGym command curves average the summed sensitivity over all subsets of each size; their error bars describe subset variability. The cell panel reports descriptive conditional medians and fit fractions; cross-model parameter-count comparisons are descriptive. The external comparisons in Supplementary~\S6 are exploratory and retain their sampling, coverage and dependence limitations. Model-population PCA, animal-feature PCA and sensitivity spectra are treated as different quantities.
